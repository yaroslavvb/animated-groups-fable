Billiards

Three balls, eighteen copies of each, and never an overlap.

Every ball on the screen is a copy of one of three, put there by a symmetry of the film. Some of those symmetries shift time as well as space, so each ball shares the plane with copies of itself running ⅙, ⅓, ½, ⅔ and ⅚ of a period behind.

Each ball carries its own clock. The colour grows out of the centre over the first half of the period and is eaten away from the centre over the second: a dot is a ball just starting, a full disk with a pinhole is one just past halfway, a thin ring is one nearly done.

Click the picture to play or pause; the arrow keys jump between the six marked instants of the loop, shift for a fine step. Symmetry axes draws mirror lines solid and glide lines dashed, and puts a triangle on every 3-centre — filled where the rotation costs no time, hollow where it also runs the film a third of a period on.

Why it cannot be simulated

The trajectories are straight between breakpoints, and each ball drifts a whole lattice vector per period, so the film loops although no ball ends where it began. The constraint is that no ball may overlap any other ball anywhere in the plane at any instant — including the copies of itself at the five other phases. The solution sits exactly on that constraint: over the whole loop the closest any two balls come is one diameter, so they do make contact, and never more than contact.

That cannot be run forward in time. An impact at time t has to deflect the partner too, and the partner is a copy of the same ball at t − ⅓, already in the past; changing it changes the collision that caused it. The whole periodic trajectory is solved at once instead — a self-consistent loop, found by relaxing the closed polylines against every clone of every ball until nothing overlaps. Because no two balls ever overlap, paint order cannot matter, so the drawn frame is exactly symmetric and not merely the point set behind it.
