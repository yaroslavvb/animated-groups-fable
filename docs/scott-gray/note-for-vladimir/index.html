Periodic Gray–Scott waves with cyclic time symmetry

Vladimir, your SymSim visualizations inspired this experiment: can a wallpaper-group rotation correspond to advancing a chemical pattern around a periodic orbit? The extension keeps the Gray–Scott reaction–diffusion law and adds constraints on the entire space–time solution. [1]

∂u/∂t = Du Δu − uv2 + F(1 − u) ∂v/∂t = Dv Δv + uv2 − (F + k)v

Here u and v are concentrations. We use constant coefficients, a flat Euclidean domain, and periodic spatial boundaries. Writing q = (u, v), the added conditions are

q(x, t + T) = q(x, t), q(gx, t + τgT) = q(x, t). g is a spatial group operation; τg is its phase offset, measured in turns of the orbit.

The offsets form a cyclic character: τgh = τg + τh (mod 1). A colour permutation becomes a shift of time for both concentrations; their species identities remain fixed. No reaction term, forcing, or time reversal is introduced. The colour palette used to display concentration is separate from this group-theoretic use of “colour.” [2]

For the 442 example g96, with short colour signature 444422 derived from Chaim’s notation, the canonical rotation R(x, y) = (−y, x) carries τ = ¼. Thus a quarter-period of evolution matches the rotated field. The viewer’s named generator α uses the inverse rotation and τ = ¾; these describe the same constraint. g97 has the same short colour orders but the opposite directed phase. [2]

L is the size of the periodic computational cell. For 442 it is the side of the square torus, in the model’s spatial units. It measures the whole translation cell, rather than the smaller rotational fundamental domain. For 632 it is the length of each basis vector of the triangular lattice, whose cell is a rhombus.

With N nodes along each direction, the grid spacing is h = L/N (called dx in the code). Here h = 256/48 ≈ 5.33. Diffusion multiplies the neighbor-difference stencil by D/h2. Equivalently, on normalized square coordinates ξ = x/L, its coefficient is D/L2. Doubling L with D fixed quarters diffusion for the same normalized pattern and changes which wavelengths fit. Increasing N at fixed L refines the same physical problem. Changing L changes the problem itself; it is independent of the viewer’s zoom or tile count. [3]

Comparing numerical scales. Your shader’s DiffR/DiffG multiply unscaled neighbor differences; our corresponding coefficients are Du/h² and Dv/h². The example uses the five-point stencil. The optional SymSim nine-point weights (0.8 axial, 0.2 diagonal, −4 centre) have a leading continuum factor of 1.2. Matching continuum diffusion across those stencils requires dividing D by 1.2 in the nine-point version, or multiplying h by √1.2. Matching F and k alone therefore does not reproduce a preset. [1] [3]

How the loops are found. For g96, we solve ΦT/4(q₀) = q₀ ∘ R⁻¹, where Φ is ordinary numerical Gray–Scott evolution. The unknowns are the initial field and T, with a phase condition fixing the arbitrary time origin. Hopf-mode seeds and continuation supply candidates; GPU batches accelerate the independent trajectories needed for Newton correction. Bulatov presets remain possible starting guesses. The current examples are concentration waves; a periodic U-skate glider has not been established. [4]

What “verified” means here. Independent, unprojected forward integration checks return, the intervening trajectory, and every requested phase relation, then repeats with a smaller timestep. The published filter also requires every nonzero-offset rotation to visibly fail as a same-time symmetry in both channels, throughout the interpolated loop. These are numerical periodic orbits of the discretized equations, with finite tolerances; they do not establish continuum existence, stability, or completeness. A failed search does not establish impossibility. [5]
