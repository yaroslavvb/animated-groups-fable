The hierarchy

Coloured wallpaper groups, space-time groups, clockwork groups, space groups, polar space groups — six classifications of the same kind of object, and exactly how they nest. Colours are counted up to six.

A looping animation of a wallpaper pattern can be described in at least six established vocabularies, and they were built by people who were not talking to each other: colour crystallographers in the 1950s–80s, space-group crystallographers since 1891, a mathematics lecturer making 16 mm films in 1956, and condensed-matter physicists after 2018. Each vocabulary has its own count. This page is an attempt to say precisely how the counts are related — which containments are genuine, which are near-misses, and where a one-to-one correspondence really does hold.

Two of the relations turn out to be exact, and both are theorems that can be checked by machine rather than believed:

Summary

Reading a film's clock phase as a third spatial coordinate embeds the 275 film groups in the 230 space groups. The map lands in the 194 non-cubic types, hits every one of them, and has fibres of size 1, 2 or 3 — which is exactly why T. J. Fletcher's 1956 count of 194 was both wrong and nearly right. Restricted to the 68 film groups that never reverse time — the clockwork groups — it is a bijection onto the 68 polar space groups.

All of it fits in one picture. Two circles, because there are two honest universes here — a pattern in the plane wearing colours, and a crystal in space — and the film groups are what connects them:

Two corrections to the obvious guess are built into that picture. Film groups are not a subset of the coloured wallpaper groups: only the clockwork ones are colourings, because a colour permutation cannot run the palette backwards. And film groups do not merely overlap the space groups — every film group is a space group, necessarily non-cubic; what fails is the other direction, since one crystal can be several films. The rest of the page is the argument for each region of the diagram.

1. Six classifications

All six describe a pattern that repeats in two spatial directions. They differ in what else the pattern is allowed to carry, and in what counts as the same.

The two entries needing a word of definition are the middle ones. A coloured wallpaper group — Wieting's chromatic plane ornament, Senechal's colour group — is a wallpaper group Γ together with a transitive action of Γ on a set of N colours, or equivalently a subgroup of index N (the stabiliser of one colour); two are the same when an affine map of the plane carries one to the other, colours permuted freely. A polar space group is one whose point group leaves some direction fixed and fixes its sense — the ten pyroelectric classes 1, 2, m, mm2, 4, 4mm, 3, 3m, 6, 6mm. A crystal in such a group can carry a spontaneous electric polarisation, because nothing in its symmetry can turn the polar axis around.

2. One object, four readings

A film group element acts on the plane and on the loop at once,

(x, y, t) ⟼ (M(x, y) + v,  st + τ),   M ∈ O(2),  s = ±1,

and the whole hierarchy is the observation that τ can be read four ways. As a phase it is a fraction of the period: the film comes back to itself after waiting τ. As a colour it is one of N labels, when the τ's that occur form a finite cyclic group of order N — the case s = +1 throughout. As a height it is a fractional translation along a third axis, turning the film into a static three-dimensional crystal. And as a time it is what makes s = −1 mean something different from a mirror. This is why the whole subject looks like crystallography with one axis singled out. Three warnings, which the rest of the page is really about. The height reading forgets which axis was time, and that is a genuine loss of information (§5). The colour reading only exists when time never reverses (§4). And the three subjects do not agree on when two patterns are the same, which is where most of the numerical discrepancies live (§7).

3. Colour, up to six

Fix a wallpaper group Γ. A homomorphism χ: Γ → ℝ/ℤ with image of order N assigns each symmetry a phase; the kernel is a normal subgroup of index N with cyclic quotient, and its cosets are the N colours. This is the strict sense in which a clockwork group is a coloured wallpaper group: it is one whose colour group is CN acting regularly on the colours, so that "which colour" and "how far round the clock" are the same question.

Three columns, three notions of colouring, N = 1…6:

The first column is Wieting's, and it is famously not monotone in N: there are more four-colour plane groups than six-colour ones, and only fourteen with five colours. The middle column keeps the colourings whose colour group is cyclic and regular. The last column is the film groups themselves. Column by column the objects get more special; row by row they get scarcer for arithmetic reasons that are visible in the last two entries.

Why five colours is empty

A clock of order 5 needs a symmetry whose phase has order 5. Point operations of the plane have order 1, 2, 3, 4 or 6 — the crystallographic restriction — so no rotation or reflection can carry a fifth of a period without violating its own relation. The phase map must therefore be trivial on the point group and live entirely on the translations, and a phase that grows linearly along the lattice is a drift: pan the camera at the right constant velocity and it is gone. Hence 0. The four five-colour cyclic colourings in the middle column are real colourings of p1, pm, pg and cm; they are just not new films.

The same argument bounds the whole table: a film's clock order is 1, 2, 3, 4 or 6 and never 5, and never more than 6 — so "up to six colours" is not a restriction we impose but the entire range there is. Here are the five clocks that exist, live:

4. What a clock can be

Time reversal is what separates the film groups from the colourings. Every element of a film group acts on the loop circle S¹ = ℝ/ℤ either as a rotation t ↦ t + τ or as a reflection t ↦ −t + τ, so the image of G in Isom(S¹) is a finite subgroup: cyclic CN if the group never reverses time, dihedral DN if it does. Sorting all 275 by that image:

The cyclic row is the 68 clockwork groups, and it is the third column of §3 — same numbers, because they are the same objects counted twice. The 17 with C1 are the 17 wallpaper groups with a clock bolted on and nothing entangled. The dihedral row is the remaining 207, and those are not colourings in the ordinary sense: their "colour permutation" includes an order-reversal of the palette, which is precisely Shubnikov's antisymmetry with the prime read as play backwards. D1 — reversal with no fractional clock at all — is the black-and-white case, and the 62 such film groups are the film-group shadow of the 46 non-trivial two-colour plane groups plus the 17 grey ones.

A caution about that last sentence

62 is not 46, nor 63, and the mismatch is not an error in either count: a film group's equivalence allows changes of frame that a two-coloured plane pattern's does not, and a film reversal may carry a spatial glide that has no analogue in a static black-and-white pattern. The correspondence is structural, not numerical. §7 takes the discrepancies apart.

5. Forgetting which axis is time

Read every phase as a height and a film group becomes a discrete group of isometries of ℝ³ with a rank-3 lattice — a space group. Which ones arise?

Theorem (checked against spglib for all 275)

The height reading maps the 275 film groups into the 230 space-group types. Its image is exactly the 194 non-cubic types: no cubic type is hit, and every non-cubic type is hit. The fibres have size 1, 2 or 3 — … types come from a single film group, … from two and … from three.

Nothing cubic can appear because a cubic point group fixes no direction, while time is a direction that every film-group operation must respect. And every non-cubic type appears because a non-cubic point group always fixes some axis, which can be nominated as the time axis. That much Fletcher saw in 1956, and he subtracted the 36 cubic groups from 230 to get 194 film groups. The subtraction is right; the conclusion is not, because the map is many-to-one: one crystal can be sliced into films in several inequivalent ways, depending on which invariant axis is called time and whether the operations reversing it are read as mirrors or as reversals.

Read that table as the correction to Fletcher. The tetragonal, trigonal and hexagonal systems have a unique high-order axis, so there is nothing to choose and film groups and space groups agree exactly: 68, 25, 27. Monoclinic always splits in two — the 2-fold axis is either the time axis or a spatial one, giving the catalog's T- and R-monoclinic systems. Orthorhombic has three mutually perpendicular axes and splits by up to three. Summing the fibres gives 275; counting the types gives Fletcher's 194.

Here is one fibre, live: one space group, three films that are not equivalent as films.

6. Clockwork groups are exactly the polar space groups

Now restrict the same map to the groups that never reverse time. In the height reading, "never reverses time" says no operation sends z ↦ −z: the point group fixes the z-axis and its direction. That is the definition of a polar space group, and the count is not a coincidence.

Theorem

The height reading restricts to a bijection between the 68 clockwork groups and the 68 polar space-group types. Every polar type is the lift of exactly one clockwork group, and no non-polar type is the lift of any.

The point groups occurring in the table below are therefore exactly the ten polar classes, and they are the ten planar point groups C1, C2, C3, C4, C6, D1, D2, D3, D4, D6 wearing their 3D names. A clockwork group's spatial part is a planar point group; a polar space group's point group is a planar point group standing on its polar axis. They are the same list.

Physically the dictionary is worth stating in both directions. A polar crystal has a distinguished axis with a distinguished sense, so it can be pyroelectric: heat it and a voltage appears along that axis. A clockwork film has a distinguished direction of time, so it can have a handedness in time: run it backwards and you get a film with a different symmetry group. The same 68 objects answer both questions, because polarity along the c-axis and irreversibility of the clock are the same condition.

Eight rows form four mirror-image pairs (…): films that are indistinguishable as coloured patterns, since a reflection of the plane exchanges them, but genuinely distinct as films, since no change of frame turns a clock that advances by +1/3 into one that advances by −1/3.

7. Where the counts disagree, and why

Compare the middle and last columns of §3 group by group and the totals do not merely shrink — for three wallpaper groups the film count is larger. Both directions have clean explanations, and between them they account for every entry.

Collapses. Over the four wallpaper groups with no rotation at all — p1, pm, pg, cm — … cyclic colourings become … film groups. Two mechanisms are at work. First, drift: with no rotation the point group fixes a direction, so a phase that increases steadily along that direction is a camera pan, and the film is the one you started with. This kills all five non-trivial colourings of p1 at a stroke. Second, migration: the height reading can turn a time offset into a spatial one. Give pm's mirror half a period and its lift is a glide plane — space-group type Pc, number 7 — which is also what pg's ordinary spatial glide lifts to. A time glide over pm and a plain glide over pg are the same crystal with the axes labelled differently, and under the crystallographic convention the catalog uses — where any unimodular re-basing of the space-time lattice is allowed — they are one film group.

So is the spatial projection an invariant?

Not under that convention: the example above is two projections, pm and pg, of one film group, and the catalog's base is a canonical representative rather than an invariant. Under the stricter equivalence — conjugation by literal changes of frame, which refuses to re-slice simultaneity — the projection is an invariant and the two are different films. This is not a second disagreement but the same one: rotation-free bases are exactly the R‑monoclinic system, which is exactly where the two conventions part company (13 → 21 types, 275 → 283 in total; see the tutorial's aside on frames). Everywhere else the two agree, and so do all the counts on this page.

Splits. The opposite happens exactly four times, over p3, p4 and p6, and always for the same reason: chirality. Plane-affine equivalence of coloured patterns allows a reflection, so a colouring and its mirror image are one class. Film equivalence does not, because reflecting the plane while keeping the direction of time turns a clock that advances by +1/N into one that advances by −1/N, and those are different films. So the one three-colour clockwork colouring of p3 with a phase on the rotation becomes two film groups, P31 and P32; likewise P41/P43, P61/P65 and P62/P64. Four colouring classes, eight film groups, four extra — precisely the four enantiomorphic pairs among the 68 polar space groups.

The bookkeeping, in one line

101 cyclic colourings − 42 over the rotation-free groups + 5 films they collapse to + 4 enantiomorphic splits = 68. It works because outside p1, pm, pg and cm the point group contains a rotation, no direction is fixed, drift has nowhere to go, and the two notions of equivalence differ only by orientation.

8. The neighbours

Four adjacent classifications that people reasonably expect to be part of this picture, with a word on where each actually sits.

Magnetic (Shubnikov) groups. The 80 magnetic plane groups — 17 ordinary, 17 grey, 46 black-white — and the 1651 magnetic space groups — 230 + 230 grey + 1191 black-white — are the two-colour theory where the colour swap was originally interpreted as time reversal. That makes them the N = 2 slice of this subject, and Zamorzaev's 1953 thesis is its true ancestor. A film group with a D1 clock is doing Shubnikov's job; a film group with a CN clock is doing Zamorzaev's P-symmetry (colour by an arbitrary quality group) with the quality group ℤ/N. What film groups add is that the colour is a continuous circle of which ℤ/N is a discrete shadow, and that the circle has an orientation.

Subperiodic groups. The 7 frieze groups (isometries of the plane with a rank-1 lattice) and the 75 rod and 80 layer groups (isometries of ℝ³ with a rank-1 and a rank-2 lattice) are the classifications for a lattice that does not fill its space. They are the right home for animations that do not loop, or that tile in only one direction; our film groups always have a rank-3 space-time lattice, one direction of which is the loop, so they are space groups and not subperiodic ones. The 1+1D case is the exception worth knowing: 13 strip groups, which are the film-group analogue of the 7 friezes and are counted on the tutorial.

Sohncke and chiral space groups. The 65 Sohncke groups (no orientation-reversing operation) are the ones a chiral molecule can crystallise in, and 22 of them — 11 enantiomorphic pairs — are chiral themselves. This is a different cut of the 230 from the polar one: polarity is about a fixed direction, chirality about a fixed orientation. They overlap without containing one another. In film terms, Sohncke means the film has no spatial mirror at any phase; polar means it never runs backwards.

Choreographic crystals. Boyle–Khoo–Smith's classification of "choreographic order" — finite configurations whose symmetries are spatial operations composed with time offsets — is the same clockwork idea for point configurations rather than lattices, and the Golubitsky–Stewart H/K theorem describes which (setwise, pointwise) symmetry pairs a time-periodic state can realise. Both are about the CN quotient of §4 without a spatial lattice underneath.
