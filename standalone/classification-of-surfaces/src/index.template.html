    Why only four

    A zipper pairs two arrows. They sit on one rim or on two. Go once round the rim: one arrow points with you and its mate against you, or both point with you. That is two choices twice.

    The two colours

    Teal is the front of the paper, sand its back. On a one-sided surface you can get from teal to sand without crossing an edge: through a crosscap's line, or along a cross-handle past its seam.

    The three numbers

    Draw any map on a surface: its Euler characteristic \(\chi\) is vertices minus edges plus faces, whatever the map. A sphere has \(\chi = 2\). A hole or a crosscap lowers \(\chi\) by 1, a handle or a cross-handle by 2: \(\chi = 2 - 2a - b - c - 2d\) for \(a\) handles, \(b\) holes, \(c\) crosscaps, \(d\) cross-handles. p. 103

    After the trades, \(\circ^{a}\ast^{b}\) is two-sided with \(\chi = 2 - 2a - b\), and \(\ast^{b}\times^{c}\) with at least one crosscap is one-sided with \(\chi = 2 - b - c\). Two different symbols differ in sides, boundaries or \(\chi\), so they are different surfaces. p. 102

    Why the book needs this

    Fold a repeating pattern up and you get its orbifold: one of these surfaces, with cone points inside and corner points on its boundaries. So the symbols \(\circ\), \(\ast\) and \(\times\) are all a signature can hold besides its numbers: wonders, kaleidoscopes and miracles. Tab 5 shows them. p. 102

    What you are doing

    The book takes a surface as finitely many triangles with zippers on their edges; polygons do as well. p. 97

    A polygon is a disc, and a disc is a sphere with a hole. That is where every piece starts: tidy. The edge of a hole is its rim: a boundary of the surface. p. 97

    A zip joins two rims, or two stretches of one rim. Go once round: one arrow points with you and its mate against you (opposite ways), or both with you (the same way). Whatever rim is not zipped stays a hole: the book calls that case gaping, and the case with nothing left snug. p. 97

    After each zip the picture is redrawn with every piece in a place of its own. Nothing is lost by that. p. 96

    The three pictures

    Each tile carries the same print, in the darker tone of its colour; the chooser in the pattern's corner changes the print, never the symmetry. Teal tiles are copies of the outlined tile that the symmetry moved without turning it over; sand tiles are its mirror images. Fold the pattern so that every tile lies on the outlined one: the sand ones land face down, the two sides of one sheet.

    What happens to the outlined tile's edges gives the surface. A mirror edge (red) stays an edge: the rim of a hole. Two edges with the same letter are zipped along their arrows, as in tab 3. Corners that meet at a point make a cone point, or a corner of the rim if they meet on a mirror.

    The Magic Theorem

    Everything in a signature has a price: \(\circ\) costs $2, \(\ast\) and \(\times\) cost $1, a number \(n\) before the star \(\frac{n-1}{n}\), a number \(n\) after a star \(\frac{n-1}{2n}\). p. 29

    • Exactly $2: a pattern on the plane. That leaves 17 signatures. p. 30
    • Under $2: a pattern on a sphere, and the change is 2 over the number of symmetries. p. 53
    • Over $2: a pattern on the hyperbolic plane. Every such signature has one. p. 237

    Any signature you can write has a price, and with the row under the signature you can write any: only a handful under $2 have no pattern. p. 237

    The price is what each thing takes off the Euler characteristic of tab 2: a sphere has 2, a handle takes 2, a hole or a crosscap 1. A cone point of order \(n\) is shared by \(n\) tiles, so it counts as \(\frac{1}{n}\) of a point and takes \(\frac{n-1}{n}\).

    The hyperbolic plane on a flat screen

    The disc is Poincaré's picture of it. Angles are true; straight lines show as arcs that meet the rim squarely; and the rim is infinitely far away, so the tiles crowding toward it are all the size of the one in the middle. Drag any of them to the middle to see. p. 221

    The chapter in four steps
    1. A surface is polygons with zippers on their edges.
    2. Zip one pair at a time. Each zip joins two pieces or makes a cap, a crosscap, a handle or a cross-handle, and may leave holes. So every surface is tidy. Tab 3 p. 96
    3. A cross-handle is two crosscaps, and next to a crosscap a handle is two more. So every surface is handles and holes, or holes and crosscaps. Tab 2 p. 97
    4. No two of these are the same surface: they differ in sides, in boundaries or in Euler characteristic. Tab 4 counts them. p. 102
