How picking any two features on an orbifold—a cone point \(p\), a composite \(22\) line, a mirror tree \({*}\dots\), a crosscap \({\times}\), or either side \(\tfrac{1}{2}o\) of a handle—sets an isolating geodesic of length \(L\) and twist \(\mathrm{tw}\), together with cuff lengths and handle fault-line shift on higher-dimensional families.

1. Vladimir Bulatov’s idea and Chaim Goodman-Strauss’s generalization

Vladimir Bulatov noted after seeing the first four-cone version that the paired 3D/2D view makes separating geodesics clear and suggested that pair separation and twist could parametrize arbitrary orbifold shapes. Chaim Goodman-Strauss had raised the caveat that a bare unordered pair of features does not uniquely name a cut (because infinitely many homotopy classes of separating loops join the same unordered pair), and then laid out the full generalization built here across all five feature types: cone points \(n\), composite pairs \(22\), mirror trees \({*}\dots\), crosscaps \({\times}\), and the two sides \(\tfrac{1}{2}o\) of each handle with its own fault-line shift.

On any four-feature group (\(3333\), \(2223\), \(333{*\!}\), \(333{*}3\), \(333{\times}\), \(o33\), or Euclidean \(2222\)), picking two features brings the unique shortest arc between them to the middle of the pattern and names the separating closed geodesic \(L\) with twist \(\mathrm{tw}\). The 3D surface acts as the topological selector: whenever you re-pick a pair, the tiling stays stationary on screen, solves for the canonical tree copy closest to the previous cut, and glides smoothly onto the new pair’s midpoint.

2. The tree and its isolating geodesic

For a hyperbolic orbifold \((p, q \mid r, s)\) cut by a closed geodesic of length \(L > 0\) separating lower cone points \(A\) (order \(p\)) and \(B\) (order \(q\)) from upper cone points \(C\) (order \(r\)) and \(D\) (order \(s\)), let \(h_A, h_B, h_C, h_D\) be their perpendicular hyperbolic distances from the cut axis, given for \(A, B\) by two Khayyam–Saccheri / Lambert relations:

\[ \begin{aligned} \sinh h_A &= \frac{\cos(\pi/q) + \cos(\pi/p)\cosh(L/2)}{\sin(\pi/p)\sinh(L/2)},\\[4pt] \sinh h_B &= \frac{\cos(\pi/p) + \cos(\pi/q)\cosh(L/2)}{\sin(\pi/q)\sinh(L/2)}. \end{aligned} \]

The hyperbolic distance \(d\) along the tree edge between \(A\) and \(B\) satisfies

\[ \begin{aligned} \cosh d &= \cosh h_A \cosh h_B \cosh\tfrac{L}{2}\\ &\quad {}- \sinh h_A \sinh h_B, \end{aligned} \]

with sharp lower bound as \(L \to 0\) (for \(3333\), \(d_0 = \ln 3 \approx 1.0986\)):

\[ d_0(p,q) = \operatorname{arcosh}\!\left(\frac{\cos(\pi/p)\cos(\pi/q) + 1}{\sin(\pi/p)\sin(\pi/q)}\right). \]

When \(p\) and \(q\) are not both \(2\), the tree geodesic through \(A, B\) and the isolating geodesic are ultraparallel. Their shortest common-perpendicular distance \(m\) drops onto the tree line \(AB\) at hyperbolic distance \(d_A\) from \(A\) (the midpoint of \(AB\) when \(p = q\)):

\[ \begin{aligned} \tanh d_A &= \frac{\cos(\pi/p)}{\tanh h_A},\\[4pt] \sinh m &= \frac{\sinh h_A}{\cosh d_A}. \end{aligned} \]

When \(p = q = 2\) (the \(2\text{–}2\) composite line-feature on \(2223\)), \(h_A = h_B = 0\) and \(m = 0\): the tree runs directly along the isolating geodesic. Walking the sharpest left-turn path \(T^{\mp k}(A, B)\) along the tree—where \(T = R_A R_B\)—converges to both rim ends of the cut, with perpendicular legs dropping from \(A, B, C, D\) onto it.

When one feature is a geodesic cuff of translation length \(b > 0\)—a plain mirror circle \({*}\) (\(b = b_*\)), a crosscap \({\times}\) with one-sided glide step \(b_\times\) (\(b = 2b_\times\)), or one side of a handle \(o\) (\(b = b_o\))—paired with a cone of order \(p\), the shortest orthogonal arc \(D\) from the cone to the cuff geodesic satisfies

\[ \begin{aligned} \sinh D &= \frac{\cosh(L/2) + \cos(\pi/p)\cosh(b/2)}{\sin(\pi/p)\sinh(b/2)},\\[4pt] \cosh h_\gamma &= \frac{\cos(\pi/p) + \cosh(L/2)\cosh(b/2)}{\sinh(L/2)\sinh(b/2)}. \end{aligned} \]

For \({*}3\), two mirror rays meet at \(\pi/3 < \pi\) inside a crown shifted outward from the separating cuff by \(w = \operatorname{arsinh}(\cos(\pi/6)/\sinh(b_*/2))\), so the shortest arc drops orthogonally onto the nearest mirror segment (between corners). When both sides \(o^+, o^-\) of a handle are paired together on \(o33\), they sit as the two equal cuffs of a one-holed torus of waist \(L\) joined by the orthogonal arc \(D_{oo}\):

\[ \cosh D_{oo} = \frac{\cosh(L/2) + \cosh^2(b_o/2)}{\sinh^2(b_o/2)}. \]

On the Euclidean pillow \(2222\) (fixed tile area \(2\)), the tree of length \(\ell\) runs between \((-\ell/2, 0)\) and \((\ell/2, 0)\), the second cone row lies \(w = 1/\ell\) above, and the isolating cut runs parallel halfway between them at distance \(m = \frac{1}{2\ell}\).

3. Twist period, half-turn swap, and the handle fault line

If \(t\) is the signed shear in hyperbolic length units along the hyperbolic cut axis of length \(L\), or \(s\) is the horizontal slide of the upper cone row along the Euclidean \(2222\) cut of closed length \(2\ell\), the dimensionless twist in turns is

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathrm{tw} &= \frac{t}{L} \in \bigl[-0.5,\; +0.5\bigr]\quad(\text{hyperbolic}),\\[4pt] \mathrm{tw} &= \frac{s}{2\ell}\quad(2222). \end{aligned} \]

Increasing \(\mathrm{tw}\) by \(1\) shifts the far pair by one full period \(L\) along the isolating geodesic (or lattice vector \(2\ell\) in \(2222\)): every feature and rigid comma returns onto its own copy, so \(\mathrm{tw} = -0.5\) and \(\mathrm{tw} = +0.5\) depict the same pattern. Shifting by half a turn (\(\mathrm{tw} \mapsto \mathrm{tw} \pm \tfrac{1}{2}\)) slides the upper row by \(L/2\) (or \(\ell\)); whenever the far pair has equal cone orders, the lattice returns to the same sites with \(C\) and \(D\) swapped, carrying their rigid comma markings across.

On \(o33\), the handle itself is a translation-invariant geodesic \(\gamma_o\) of closed length \(b_o > 0\) whose two sides \(o^+\) and \(o^-\) glue along a fault line. The extra Handle shift parameter \(\tau_o \in [-0.5, +0.5]\) turns translates \(o^-\) against \(o^+\) along \(\gamma_o\) (drawn in blue on every tile with matching ticks on each side), also with exact period \(1\) turn. Together \((L, \mathrm{tw}, b_o, \tau_o)\) span all \(4\) Fenchel–Nielsen coordinates of \(o33\).

4. Parameters compared with SymDraw and Orbipix

Both SymmHub / SymDraw and Orbipix control two-handle wallpaper and hyperbolic orbifolds using neck coordinates. In SymDraw for hyperbolic \(3333\), tube_1_l measures the hyperbolic closed-geodesic waist \(L\) along the cut, while tube_1_t is the hyperbolic translation distance \(t = \mathrm{tw}\cdot L\) along it (the interface wraps at \(\pm 0.5\) hyperbolic units rather than turns, with the opposite sign convention to \(2222\); SymmHub’s conePair and handle controls use fractional turns). For Euclidean \(2222\), SymmHub’s Orbifold mode (tube_1_l, tube_1_t) and Orbipix (lattice_b, lattice_c in iGroup_2222(1, b, c) with base vector \(a = 1\)) relate to this page’s normalized frame \((\ell, \mathrm{tw})\) by \(b = 2\ell^2\) and \(c = -\ell^2\,\mathrm{tw} = -\tfrac{b}{2}\,\mathrm{tw}\).

Notation follows Conway, Burgiel, and Goodman-Strauss, The Symmetries of Things (2008).

5. All five feature kinds and carving arbitrary orbifolds

Chaim Goodman-Strauss’s separation construction generalizes unchanged to all five topological building blocks: cone points \(n\), composite pairs \(22\) (whose tree degenerates into a straight line along the isolating cut), boundary/mirror trees \({*}n_1n_2\dots\), crosscaps \({\times}\) (a glide-reflection axis geodesic), and the two oriented sides \(o^+, o^-\) of a handle geodesic. In every case, picking two features extracts the shortest connecting arc between the point, geodesic, or convex mirror tree of each feature; repeating all symmetric copies builds a planar tree whose ends converge to the unique separating geodesic, placing Length and Twist cleanly at the center of the view.

Two mathematical points behind this picture are worth noting explicitly:

No constraint on \(o\) beyond simplicity: Any simple non-separating closed geodesic on the orbifold acts as a handle geodesic \(\gamma_o\). There is no upper or lower bound on its length \(b_o > 0\) nor any restriction on the fault-line shift \(\tau_o\)—the only geometric requirement is that its lifts remain pairwise disjoint so the curve on the orbifold does not self-intersect.

Generic uniqueness vs. symmetric tie walls: Inside a fixed homotopy class of arc on the orbifold, the shortest geodesic between two points, geodesics, or convex mirror trees is strictly unique. Globally (when optimizing over all lifts to select the canonical tile copy), it is unique for generic shapes, while symmetric loci form lower-dimensional tie walls—for example a square \(2222\) lattice where two diagonals have equal length, a half-turn on equal cones, or \(333{*}3\) at \(\tau_c = 0\) where adjacent mirror facets tie at equal distance.

Because each geometric picture already fixes every arc length and twist on its newly exposed cuffs, carving an arbitrary higher-genus or \(k \ge 5\)-feature orbifold recursively—peeling off two features along their separating geodesic in any order—carries known parameters at every step. Broader non-recursive families also appear in Classification of Surfaces and Orbifold Showcase.
