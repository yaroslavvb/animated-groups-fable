How picking two features on a four-cone orbifold sets two Fenchel–Nielsen handles—the cut length \(L\) (or pair spacing \(\ell\) on \(2222\), closed length \(2\ell\)) and twist \(\mathrm{tw}\)—and why both are needed to navigate shapes of \(3333\), \(2223\), and \(2222\).

1. Vladimir Bulatov’s idea and Chaim Goodman-Strauss’s caveat

Vladimir Bulatov suggested visualising how picking any pair of cone points on an orbifold’s 3D surface controls its isolating cut and twist handles; Chaim Goodman-Strauss added the caveat that a bare unordered pair of cone points does not uniquely name a cut. Built from their explanations as Yaroslav Bulatov understood them; neither has seen this build.

On any of the four-cone sphere orbifolds \(3333\), \(2223\), or \(2222\), a symmetry group with a single cone pair picked out has two real handles—the length of the simple closed geodesic separating that pair from the other two cones, and the twist along it. Because infinitely many homotopy classes of separating loops join the same unordered pair (distinguished by which arc, or tree edge, connects them), the 3D dodecahedron ball serves as a topological selector: whenever you re-pick a pair, the pattern stays stationary on screen, solves for the canonical tree copy closest to the previous cut, and glides smoothly onto the new pair’s midpoint.

2. The tree and its isolating geodesic

For a hyperbolic orbifold \((p, q \mid r, s)\) cut by a closed geodesic of length \(L > 0\) separating lower cone points \(A\) (order \(p\)) and \(B\) (order \(q\)) from upper cone points \(C\) (order \(r\)) and \(D\) (order \(s\)), let \(h_A, h_B, h_C, h_D\) be their perpendicular hyperbolic distances from the cut axis, given for \(A, B\) by two Khayyam–Saccheri / Lambert relations:

\[ \begin{aligned} \sinh h_A &= \frac{\cos(\pi/q) + \cos(\pi/p)\cosh(L/2)}{\sin(\pi/p)\sinh(L/2)},\\[4pt] \sinh h_B &= \frac{\cos(\pi/p) + \cos(\pi/q)\cosh(L/2)}{\sin(\pi/q)\sinh(L/2)}. \end{aligned} \]

The hyperbolic distance \(d\) along the tree edge between \(A\) and \(B\) satisfies

\[ \begin{aligned} \cosh d &= \cosh h_A \cosh h_B \cosh\tfrac{L}{2}\\ &\quad {}- \sinh h_A \sinh h_B, \end{aligned} \]

with sharp lower bound as \(L \to 0\) (for \(3333\), \(d_0 = \ln 3 \approx 1.0986\)):

\[ d_0(p,q) = \operatorname{arcosh}\!\left(\frac{\cos(\pi/p)\cos(\pi/q) + 1}{\sin(\pi/p)\sin(\pi/q)}\right). \]

When \(p\) and \(q\) are not both \(2\), the tree geodesic through \(A, B\) and the isolating geodesic are ultraparallel (never meeting even if extended). Their shortest common-perpendicular distance \(m\) drops onto the tree line \(AB\) at hyperbolic distance \(d_A\) from \(A\) (the midpoint of \(AB\) when \(p = q\)):

\[ \begin{aligned} \tanh d_A &= \frac{\cos(\pi/p)}{\tanh h_A},\\[4pt] \sinh m &= \frac{\sinh h_A}{\cosh d_A}. \end{aligned} \]

When \(p = q = 2\) (on the \(2\text{–}2\) pairs of \(2223\)), \(h_A = h_B = 0\), so \(m = 0\): the tree lies directly on the isolating geodesic (drawn with an amber dashed line atop the ink tree). Walking the sharpest left-turn path \(T^{\mp k}(A, B)\) for integer steps \(k \ge 0\) along the tree—where \(T = R_A R_B\) is the product of cone rotations about \(A\) and \(B\)—converges to the two rim ends of the cut, with perpendicular legs \(h_A, h_B, h_C, h_D\) dropping from \(A, B, C, D\) onto it.

On the Euclidean pillow \(2222\) (fixed tile area \(2\)), the tree of length \(\ell\) runs between \((-\ell/2, 0)\) and \((\ell/2, 0)\), the second cone row lies \(w = 1/\ell\) above, and the isolating cut runs parallel halfway between them at distance \(m = \frac{1}{2\ell}\).

3. Twist period and the half-turn swap

If \(t\) is the signed shear in hyperbolic length units along the hyperbolic cut axis of length \(L\), or \(s\) is the horizontal slide of the upper cone row along the Euclidean \(2222\) cut of closed length \(2\ell\), the dimensionless twist in turns is

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathrm{tw} &= \frac{t}{L} \in \bigl[-0.5,\; +0.5\bigr]\quad(\text{hyperbolic}),\\[4pt] \mathrm{tw} &= \frac{s}{2\ell}\quad(2222). \end{aligned} \]

Increasing \(\mathrm{tw}\) by \(1\) shifts the upper cones \(C, D\) by one full period \(T(z) = e^{-L} z\) of the isolating geodesic (in upper half-plane coordinates \(z\) with the isolating axis along the positive imaginary axis, or full lattice vector \(2\ell\) in \(2222\)): every cone point and every local comma motif maps back onto its own copy, so \(\mathrm{tw} = -0.5\) and \(\mathrm{tw} = +0.5\) depict the same pattern.

Shifting by half a turn (\(\mathrm{tw} \mapsto \mathrm{tw} \pm \tfrac{1}{2}\)) slides the upper row rigidly by \(L/2\) (or \(\ell\) in \(2222\)). Whenever the far pair has equal cone orders (\(r = s\), as in \(3333\), \(2222\), and the \((3,2\mid 2,2)\) cut of \(2223\)), the cone lattice returns to the same sites with \(C\) and \(D\) swapped—carrying their attached rigid comma markings across (on \(3333\), the small comma slides onto \(D\)’s old site while \(C\)’s old site becomes blank, and each cone’s direction toward its former partner points opposite). Negating \(\mathrm{tw} \mapsto -\mathrm{tw}\) gives the mirror orbifold.

4. Parameters compared with SymDraw and Orbipix

Both SymmHub / SymDraw and Orbipix control two-handle wallpaper and hyperbolic orbifolds using neck coordinates. In SymDraw for hyperbolic \(3333\), tube_1_l measures the hyperbolic closed-geodesic waist \(L\) along the cut, while tube_1_t is the hyperbolic translation distance \(t = \mathrm{tw}\cdot L\) along it (the interface wraps at \(\pm 0.5\) hyperbolic units rather than turns, with the opposite sign convention to \(2222\); SymmHub’s conePair and handle controls use fractional turns). For Euclidean \(2222\), SymmHub’s Orbifold mode (tube_1_l, tube_1_t) and Orbipix (lattice_b, lattice_c in iGroup_2222(1, b, c) with base vector \(a = 1\)) relate to this page’s normalized frame \((\ell, \mathrm{tw})\) by \(b = 2\ell^2\) and \(c = -\ell^2\,\mathrm{tw} = -\tfrac{b}{2}\,\mathrm{tw}\).

Notation follows Conway, Burgiel, and Goodman-Strauss, The Symmetries of Things (2008).

5. Scope — why five cones, mirrors, handles, and crosscaps sit outside

For a sphere orbifold with four cone points, Thurston’s dimension formula leaves \(2(4 - 3) = 2\) real Fenchel–Nielsen moduli: one simple closed curve separates \((2\text{ cones})\) from \((2\text{ cones})\), producing one Length and one Twist handle. With \(k \ge 5\) cones there are \(k - 3 \ge 2\) independent separating curves (\(2k - 6\) parameters), so two handles no longer suffice.

Three-cone spherical and triangle groups (such as \(332\), \(432\), \(532\)) are rigid with \(0\) continuous parameters. Groups with mirror lines (∗), handles (o), or crosscaps (×) cut along arcs ending on mirrors or non-orientable cuffs rather than clean cone pairs. Those wider families live in the companion pages Classification of Surfaces and Orbifold Showcase.
