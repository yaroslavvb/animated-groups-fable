1. Vladimir Bulatov’s idea and Chaim Goodman-Strauss’s generalization

Vladimir Bulatov noted after seeing the first four-cone version that the paired 3D/2D view makes separating geodesics clear and suggested that pair separation and twist could parametrize arbitrary orbifold shapes. Chaim Goodman-Strauss had raised the caveat that a bare unordered pair of features does not uniquely name a cut (because infinitely many homotopy classes of separating loops join the same unordered pair), and then laid out the full generalization built here across all five feature types: cone points , composite pairs , mirror trees , crosscaps , and the two sides of each handle with its own fault-line shift.

On any four-feature group (, , , , , , or Euclidean ), picking two features brings the unique shortest arc between them to the middle of the pattern and names the separating closed geodesic with twist . The 3D surface acts as the topological selector: whenever you re-pick a pair, the tiling stays stationary on screen, solves for the canonical tree copy closest to the previous cut, and glides smoothly onto the new pair’s midpoint.

5. All five feature kinds and carving arbitrary orbifolds

Chaim Goodman-Strauss’s separation construction generalizes unchanged to all five topological building blocks: cone points , composite pairs (whose tree degenerates into a straight line along the isolating cut), boundary/mirror trees , crosscaps (a glide-reflection axis geodesic), and the two oriented sides of a handle geodesic. In every case, picking two features extracts the shortest connecting arc between the point, geodesic, or convex mirror tree of each feature; repeating all symmetric copies builds a planar tree whose ends converge to the unique separating geodesic, placing Length and Twist cleanly at the center of the view.

Two mathematical points behind this picture are worth noting explicitly:

No constraint on beyond simplicity: Any simple non-separating closed geodesic on the orbifold acts as a handle geodesic . There is no upper or lower bound on its length nor any restriction on the fault-line shift —the only geometric requirement is that its lifts remain pairwise disjoint so the curve on the orbifold does not self-intersect.

Generic uniqueness vs. symmetric tie walls: Inside a fixed homotopy class of arc on the orbifold, the shortest geodesic between two points, geodesics, or convex mirror trees is strictly unique. Globally (when optimizing over all lifts to select the canonical tile copy), it is unique for generic shapes, while symmetric loci form lower-dimensional tie walls—for example a square lattice where two diagonals have equal length, a half-turn on equal cones, or at where adjacent mirror facets tie at equal distance.

Because each geometric picture already fixes every arc length and twist on its newly exposed cuffs, carving an arbitrary higher-genus or -feature orbifold recursively—peeling off two features along their separating geodesic in any order—carries known parameters at every step.
