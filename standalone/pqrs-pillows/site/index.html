2. The solid

Take three loops, one for each pairing, any two meeting twice (§3). Their six seals do not cross: they cut the orbifold into four geodesic triangles with the cone points as corners, glued along their sides as the faces of a tetrahedron. A hyperbolic triangle is fixed by its three sides. So a tetrahedron of hyperbolic 3-space with the same six edge lengths has that very surface: the orbifold, creased along the six seals, with nothing stretched.

They exist when this matrix has three positive eigenvalues and one negative, or two positive and a zero: then the solid is flat, a doubled quadrilateral. Alexandrov's theorem says every metric of curvature on the sphere with cone angles under is the surface of exactly one convex polyhedron of hyperbolic space, flat ones included. So the solid belongs to the orbifold, not to a choice of loops, and it is the same whichever card is touched.

Only one triangle of loops has its seals along the edges: the one in which no loop is longer than its twin (§3), because an edge is the shortest way between its ends. The page reaches it from the cards' three loops by exchanging a loop for its twin while the twin is shorter; in 360 random states the tetrahedron existed every time.

It is shown in the Poincaré ball, the vertices' middle at the centre, at twice the ball's scale so that lengths near the middle are true: for . Planes are spheres there: the faces are caps, the edges arcs of circles, and the corner at is as sharp as its cone angle . The solid is flat exactly when the orbifold has a mirror through its four cone points: at Twist and of a loop that runs round two of its edges. Every preset opens there. Away from the mirror the thickness grows like , so a small Twist already makes a full tetrahedron.

Ordinary space has no such surface. If two smooth faces meet at an angle strictly between and along a curve that is a geodesic of both, then is parallel to both normals, so : the crease is straight. A tetrahedron with faces of curvature creased along geodesics would have straight edges, hence faces with the Euclidean angle sum , against Gauss–Bonnet.
